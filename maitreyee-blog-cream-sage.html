Every great ghostwriter has one superpower: the ability to disappear.

Not literally — but on the page. When you read a LinkedIn post, a thought leadership article, or a bestselling memoir written by a ghostwriter, you should never know they were there. It should sound exactly like the person whose name is on it.

That's the art of voice-matching — and it's the most valuable skill a ghostwriter can develop.

In this post, I'm sharing the exact framework I use to write in someone else's voice — whether I'm ghostwriting LinkedIn content, blog articles, or long-form thought leadership pieces. If you're an aspiring ghostwriter, a freelance content creator, or a brand founder looking to understand this process, this is for you.

1 Step One

The Voice Audit — Before You Write a Single Word

Most ghostwriters make the mistake of jumping straight into writing. That's backwards.

Before I write anything for a client, I do a Voice Audit. This means collecting and studying 10–20 samples of their existing writing or speech. I look for:

  • Sentence length — Short punchy bursts or long flowing sentences?
  • Vocabulary level — Industry jargon, casual language, or in between?
  • Tone — Warm and personal, authoritative and formal, or witty and conversational?
  • Recurring phrases — Every person has verbal tics they habitually reach for.
  • Storytelling style — Do they open with data, anecdotes, opinions, or questions?
  • Punctuation habits — Em dashes, ellipses, exclamation points — these tell you a lot.

The goal is to build a mental "voice fingerprint" of your client before you write a single word.

Pro Tip — AI Accelerator

I feed my client's existing content into an AI tool and ask it to identify patterns in their writing style. It surfaces things I might miss — and dramatically cuts down research time. AI doesn't replace the voice audit; it enhances it.

The goal is to build a mental "voice fingerprint" of your client before you write a single word.

2 Step Two

The Discovery Interview — Mining for Gold

Written samples only tell you half the story. The other half lives in how your client talks.

I always schedule a 30–60 minute discovery call before starting any ghostwriting project. But I don't use it to discuss deliverables — I use it to listen.

Questions I Ask on Every Discovery Call

  • Tell me about a moment that changed how you think about your work."
  • What do most people in your industry get wrong?"
  • What do you wish more people understood about what you do?"
  • What's a belief you hold that some people would disagree with?"

These aren't random questions. They're designed to elicit natural, unguarded speech — the voice that lives underneath the "professional" filter. I record the call (with permission) and refer back to it constantly.

3 Step Three

Build a Voice Style Guide

After the audit and discovery call, I create a one-page Voice Style Guide for every client. It lives in my working folder and I reference it before writing anything.

  • Tone words — e.g., "authoritative but warm, never preachy"
  • Sentence rhythm — e.g., "mix short punchy sentences with longer analytical ones"
  • Vocabulary list — words they use frequently + words they never use
  • Signature phrases or recurring expressions
  • Taboo topics or angles to avoid
  • Sample sentence — a before/after showing their voice vs. generic writing

This document becomes the single source of truth for every piece I write for that client.

4 Step Four

Write a "Voice Draft" — Not a Perfect Draft

Here's where most ghostwriters get stuck: they try to write a perfect first draft. That's not the goal.

The first draft is a Voice Draft. Your only job is to sound like the client — not to be brilliant.

I write the draft, then read it aloud asking: "Could my client have said this out loud, naturally?" If a sentence sounds too polished, too generic, or too much like me — I rewrite it.

A useful exercise: after writing a paragraph, ask yourself — "Would my client say 'nevertheless' or 'but here's the thing'?" The answer tells you everything.
5 Step Five

The Feedback Loop — Every Edit Is a Clue

When a client edits your draft, most ghostwriters feel deflated. I feel excited.

Every edit is a window into their voice. If they crossed out "leverage" and wrote "use" — note that. If they added an exclamation mark you didn't include — note that. If they softened your assertive opening — note that.

I update my Voice Style Guide after every feedback round. By the third or fourth piece, I can often write in a client's voice with near-zero revisions.

Every edit is a window into your client's voice. Treat feedback like gold — because it is.

Common Pitfalls

3 Voice-Matching Mistakes to Avoid

01
Writing how you think they should sound — not how they actually sound.

The Fix
Study their real content. Not their industry. Not their competitors. Them.

02
Over-polishing. Generic "professional" writing sounds like no one.

The Fix
Imperfection is often authentic. If your client is casual, be casual.

03
Ignoring spoken patterns. People write differently than they talk.

The Fix
Always listen to recordings, podcasts, or interviews of your client if available.

Final Thought

The Best Ghostwriters Are Empaths First

Voice-matching isn't just a technical skill. It's an act of deep listening.

The best ghostwriters I know are endlessly curious about other people — how they think, how they see the world, what matters to them. That curiosity is what makes the words feel real.

Master that, and you won't just be a ghostwriter. You'll be a trusted creative partner — the kind clients keep for years.
